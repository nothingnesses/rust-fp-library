## Project Principles

1. Prefer the cleaner long-term architecture over compatibility with existing implementation details: prioritise correctness, internal coherence, technical debt reduction, and maintainability, and when a local compatibility-preserving fix conflicts with a cleaner architecture, choose the cleaner architecture unless a concrete limitation prevents it.
2. API-breaking changes are acceptable when they lead to a better end state.
3. When a concrete limitation forces a fallback from the preferred approach, document the limitation, the trade-offs, and the fallback before adopting it.
4. Make illegal states unrepresentable. Work out the valid states and transitions first, then encode them using algebraic data types (e.g., enums, structs, newtypes) and type-level constraints, so invalid combinations cannot be constructed, instead of admitting them and guarding against them with defensive run-time checks. Express the valid states and transitions with tools like state diagrams and decision tables; an exhaustive decision table over a product of sum types is a total pattern match. When a concrete limitation of the language or type system prevents encoding a constraint directly, fall back to the closest approximation it allows, backed by a checked boundary or a documented invariant, and record the limitation per principle 3.
5. Parse, don't validate (Alexis King). Reject malformed input at the boundary and turn it into a precise type, so the pure core can trust the data it holds rather than re-checking it or adding fallbacks deeper in. Validation lives once, at the boundary (the effectful shell); everything inside operates on already-valid types.
6. Make failure and absence explicit, and pattern matches total. Represent failure and absence as data the caller must engage with (Result and Option, not nulls, exceptions, or silent defaults), and prefer exhaustive matches with no catch-all wildcard where a missing case would be a bug, so adding a variant forces every decision point to be revisited.
7. Ground decisions and implementations in evidence. Before a recommended approach is adopted and built out as a complete feature, validate it with a small proof-of-concept that demonstrates it builds and produces the expected outputs. If validation fails, adopt the next-best candidate and validate that too, repeating until an approach is validated or the candidate approaches are exhausted; if they are exhausted, surface the impasse for a decision rather than forcing through an unvalidated approach.

## Documentation Protocol

This plan intentionally does not use a separate section for previously resolved decisions. Resolved or adopted decisions are folded into the implementation plan as concrete steps. This keeps the document from accumulating stale decision history while still making the intended work clear. Correspondingly, when an entry in the Open Questions section is resolved, it is removed from that section entirely and its decision is folded into the relevant Implementation Step; it is not retained in the Open Questions section as a compressed "resolved, see step X" pointer, since such pointers re-accumulate the stale decision history this protocol exists to avoid. When no entries remain, the Open Questions section simply states that none are open.

During implementation, this document should be kept up to date by indicating, for each of the implementation steps, what their current status is.

Status convention and the step lifecycle: each work item carries a `Status:` line (`not started`, `in progress`, `blocked on <item or question>`, `decided`, `complete`, or `rejected with reason`). A step passes through the lifecycle below, and stale narration is removed at each transition rather than allowed to accumulate:

- While a step is not started or in progress, it is a numbered entry carrying its recommendation or plan text and a short stable name (see the naming convention below).
- When a step completes, any of its substance that a live step or the current-state summary still depends on is folded forward into that live location, so the live prose stays self-contained; the step's own play-by-play is then removed, because it lives in git, reachable from the implementing commits, and keeping it in the plan only re-accumulates the stale history this protocol exists to avoid. When the substance cannot be folded cleanly, the step falls back to being compressed in place to the adopted decision plus its implementing-commit pointer rather than removed (per principle 3, the concrete-limitation fallback).
- When every step of an item is done, the item remains (it is the work that was to be done) but its step list collapses to a `Status: complete` line naming the implementing commits, so the item stays traceable to the code without narrating each step.

This lifecycle applies only to the Implementation Steps; an Open Questions entry is removed outright when it is resolved (per the rule above), never compressed to a pointer there. The breakage guard is mechanical: removing a step and resolving every reference to it happen in the same change, and a grep for references to the removed step must come back empty before that change is committed.

Numbering and naming convention: the plan has two tiers with opposite rules, and conflating them is the failure this convention prevents.

The top-level entries (items 1 through 21) are stable identities. Their numbers are permanent labels, not positions: items are never resequenced, their numbers do not track execution order (item 21 executes between Phases B and C, and items 2 and 3 execute after item 11), and they are the anchor that frozen prior-generation records cite from outside this document. An item is referenced by its number.

The numbered steps within an item are the sequenced tier. A step number encodes execution order and nothing else. An ordinal cannot serve as both a position in a sequence and an identity for cross-references, because the two disagree the moment a step is resequenced, so each live step also carries a short stable name (written as a bold lead-in, for example `**Custom-effects guide.**`) and all prose, including the item's own `Status:` line, refers to that name rather than the number. Keeping identity in the name lets steps be reordered freely for execution-order accuracy without any reference going stale, since a name goes stale only when a step is renamed or deleted (rare) whereas a number goes stale on every reorder. Naming matters only while a step is live: once it is done the step is removed per the lifecycle above, so no long-lived reference to a step number survives either way. This is a documented invariant maintained by review, not a tooling-checked boundary: the steps are ordered-list items rather than headers, so the link checker (which resolves fragments against header slugs only) cannot verify a by-name reference; naming was still chosen over numbering because the number is the more fragile of the two.

Work that is not part of the sequence at all, standing context or obligations rather than ordered actions (for example a "Foundation-sweep impact" or "Documentation debt to settle here" note), is an appendix, not a numbered step, so it is never mistaken for something to do now.

## Open Questions, Decisions, Issues and Blockers

**OQ-18B: multi-shot async, the staged written decision.** Item 18 requires a written decision, before any multi-shot (Rc/Arc) async implementation, between cloneable cached (`Shared`-style) futures so multi-shot stores can re-await and documenting async as single-shot-only. The approaches:

1. Adopt `Shared`-style cached futures now: wrap the `Await` cell's future in a memoizing, cloneable shape (futures-util's `Shared` is the reference shape; a hand-rolled cache over a shared cell is the no-dependency form), bounding the awaited output `Clone`, so a re-entered continuation re-awaits the cached result. Trade-offs: it builds machinery whose only consumer is the multi-shot interpretation round, which has not started, so no proof of concept can validate it end to end (against principle 7); it silently commits to memoize-once semantics (every re-entry sees the one awaited value) before that round decides whether re-entered effects replay or memoize, which is exactly the semantic question the round owns; and it adds a dependency or bespoke cache today for a consumer that may want the other semantics.
2. Document async as single-shot-only, with the revisit pinned to the multi-shot interpretation round. The `Await` cell stores `Pin<Box<dyn Future>>`, which is not `Clone`, while multi-shot re-entry demands duplicable structure (the `Clone` bounds the Rc/Arc store family already enforces, pinned by the `free_rc_bind_requires_clone` and `coyoneda_not_clone` compile-fail cases), so the single-shot boundary is enforced at the type level today; this approach makes it written policy and names the two candidate mechanisms for the revisit: `Shared`-style memoize-once (one await, the cached value shared across re-entries, output `Clone`) and thunked re-await (the cell stores a future thunk, each re-entry re-executes the async operation). Choosing between them is the multi-shot round's memoize-versus-replay decision in async form, made when its stores and runners exist to validate against.

Recommended: approach 2, judged against the principles: principle 7 forbids building the unvalidatable half now (no consumer exists to prove either semantics right), and principle 3 is satisfied by recording the limitation, the trade-off, and the revisit trigger in writing; the module and guide documentation already state the single-shot scope (the local non-`Send` boxed future, the single-shot `Box` store), so the deliverable is this policy record itself, folded into item 18.

Per the Documentation Protocol above, resolved and adopted decisions are folded into the Implementation Steps as concrete steps (carrying their evidence and implementing commits) rather than retained here, so this section holds only items still awaiting a decision. For navigation to the decisions already made: the strategic choice that organises the plan (adopt the unified row, FS-1) is the foundation of Phase B and item 4; the build-readiness decisions raised while implementing item 4's `Store`-parameterised substrate are folded into item 4's Status as adopted decisions (the OQ-6 series); and the bounded follow-ups that choice left open are tracked on their own items (per-`Store` construction generation in item 7; the exponential higher-order-effect round in items 18 and 19).

### 2. Run-level test hardening: stack safety and laws

Findings: architecture.md section 3.8 (no deep `Run` program tests; `stack_safety.rs` covers other types); coverage-gaps.md section 5 (laws untested).

Decision (adopted): targeted test suites (deep-program tests on the order of 100k steps through the FS-1 interpreter, its interpose walkers, and row-`embed` widening, at the Box store and at the multi-shot store once Phase D lands it, plus Functor/Monad law tests on small concrete rows and `embed` naturality against interpreter results), with a random well-typed-program generator held as a contingency pursued only if the targeted tests start missing classes of bugs. The open risks are specific (recursion over program depth hiding in traversals; queue rotation under deep continuation chains) and targeted tests reach them at a fraction of a generator's cost, which would itself be a project to build over type-level rows.

Aim-point (the OQ-2A decision, adopted): the suites are written against the public surface, a test-defined row via `define_row!`/`define_effect!` and a hand-written interpreter in the shape the custom-effects guide teaches (an iterative `Free::resume` loop, recursion only per higher-order nesting), because no public generic interpreter exists (the OQ-11E finding) and the properties under test are owned by the substrate and the elaboration recursion contract, not by the `pub(crate)` conformance fixture; the reference interpreter keeps one cheap in-module depth case as the contract's reference implementation, and the slice's interpose walkers keep their documented single-layer scope (the deep-interpose property is pinned against a hand-written iterative public walker instead). Placing the suites in-module was rejected (item 3's benches cannot reach `pub(crate)`, and the deep-interpose case is inexpressible against single-layer-by-design fixtures); deferring until the generic runner exists was rejected (it withholds exactly the hardening and baselines the runner design round should build on).

Status: complete (commits `5d62bee9` and `1a950fcf` on `feat/effects-fs1`, each with `just verify` and the effects-off build and test exit 0). The deep-program suite (`fp-library/tests/effects_deep_programs.rs`) drives seven public-surface cases at 100k depth: left- and right-associated `bind` chains; a lazy row-widening walk and a lazy interpose-shaped rewriting walk, each deferring its recursive step into the continuations via the row's `Coyoneda`-backed `Functor` so native stack per step is constant; a deep program under one elaborated catch (the CatList rotation case); a deep aborting action whose recovery keeps its shared-counter steps; and the elaboration recursion contract (a full-depth program under one hundred nested catches passes, native stack growing with nesting, not length); plus the two in-module reference-interpreter depth cases. The law tests (`fp-library/tests/effects_laws.rs`) check the functor laws, the monad laws (with effectful Kleisli arrows), and `embed` naturality by observational equality (interpretation results and handler-state traces) over a seeded program set through the same public loop. Together they pin the POC-3 property (deep programs run and drop safely) at the effects shapes over the unified row.

### 3. Benchmark expansion and the tail-resumptive investigation

Findings: coverage-gaps.md section 5 (benchmark gaps); refactoring-opportunities.md R11; external-ideas.md items 1 and 6 (EvEff/koka taxonomy; corophage's dispatch-position calibration).

### 9. Tagged (labelled) effects

Findings: coverage-gaps.md sections 2, 3, and 4 candidate 1 (no mechanism for duplicate same-type effects; both references treat labels as table stakes).

Decision (adopted, folded into dispatch): under FS-1 membership is brand-keyed (the brand is the label), which the sweep's grounding confirmed against heftia (`LabelOf`-resolved `:>` membership) and POC-2 realises as type-level brand search.
